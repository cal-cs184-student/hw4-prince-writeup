Overview

We built a cloth simulator by connecting a grid of point masses with springs. At each substep, the simulator computes forces, advances the masses with Verlet integration, resolves contacts with the cloth itself and with scene objects, and limits spring stretch. A triangle mesh follows the masses and lets us render the same simulation with different GLSL materials.

The parameter comparisons show how different pieces of the simulator control what we see. Spring stiffness and mass affect how far the cloth sags, while damping mostly changes how the motion dies out. The shader comparisons show that changing the surface normals can create a convincing texture, while moving vertices changes the outline as well.

Part 1: Masses and Springs

In buildGrid() we lay out the masses in row-major order, with spacing equal to the cloth width and height divided by the number of intervals along each axis. Horizontal cloth starts in the xz-plane at y = 1. Vertical cloth starts in the xy-plane with a small random z offset in [−0.001, 0.001], which breaks symmetry when it falls. Scene-provided column and row pairs mark the pinned masses. In pinned2.json, the grid is 50 × 50 and the pins are at (0, 0) and (49, 0).

After creating all masses, we add springs in a second pass so their pointers refer to the finished grid. Structural springs connect to the immediate left and upper neighbors. Shearing springs connect to the two upper diagonals. Bending springs connect two columns left and two rows up. Checking those directions visits each spring once. Each spring stores the initial distance between its endpoints as its rest length.

Part 2: Simulation via Numerical Integration

At each substep, we assign every point mass the sum of the external forces ma, which clears the old forces. Each mass is the cloth area times the scene density divided by the number of masses. Each enabled spring contributes a Hooke force proportional to its extension from rest, along the line joining its endpoints. We add equal and opposite contributions to the endpoints. Structural and shearing springs use the configured ks, and bending springs use 0.2 × ks.

Verlet integration estimates motion from the current and previous positions. With d equal to the damping percentage divided by 100 and Δt equal to 1 / (frames per second × substeps per frame), each unpinned mass advances by

xnext = x + (1 − d)(x − xprev) + (F / m) Δt2.

Large spring extensions can make the cloth look rubbery. After self-collision and object collision, a separate pass checks each stored spring and shortens it if it exceeds 110% of its rest length. The pass splits the correction evenly if both endpoints can move, gives the full correction to the free endpoint if the other is pinned, and skips springs with two pinned endpoints. In this implementation the spring toggles select force contributions and wireframe visibility, while the stretch-limit pass visits every stored spring.

Spring stiffness

In pinned2.json, the free edge swings downward as the sheet falls from horizontal. At ks = 500, the springs give more under gravity, and the hanging cloth develops a deep dip and pronounced folds below the pins. The default ks = 5000 gives a shallower dip as the swing settles. At ks = 50000, the springs resist extension and bending more strongly. The hanging sheet is flatter, with the edge between the pins nearly straight.

Density

Density changes the mass of every grid point. The gravity term still gives the same free-fall acceleration because mg is divided by m. With ks fixed, the spring force produces less acceleration on a heavier point mass, so the cloth deforms more under its weight as it swings and hangs from the pins. The heavy wireframe shows a deeper arc and more curvature below the top edge than the default. The light wireframe hangs with a shallower dip. Captions use the scene density, which is ten times the GUI reading.

Damping

Damping scales the displacement carried over from the previous substep. At 0%, fine ripples and a curl along the free edges remain visible after the sheet swings down. At the default 0.2%, the moving sheet shows broader curvature, and the oscillations die out as it settles. At about 0.667%, the swing and ripples are more strongly damped. Once motion dies out, the same spring and gravity forces govern the hanging shape.

Part 3: Handling Collisions with Other Objects

Sphere. For a free mass inside or on the sphere, we normalize the center-to-mass vector and scale it by the radius to find the corresponding surface point. The implementation skips the exact center, where this direction is undefined. From the previous position, it applies the vector toward that surface point scaled by (1 − friction). In sphere.json the friction is 0.3. The displayed sphere uses 92% of the collision radius to reduce visible intersections with the rendered triangles.

Plane. We take the dot product of the plane normal with the displacement from a point on the plane to each of the previous and current positions. A negative product of those signed distances indicates a crossing. We project the current position onto the plane and offset the contact by 0.0001 along the normal toward the side the mass came from. We use the same (1 − friction) factor to scale the vector from the previous position to this target.

The normal-shaded results show how stiffness changes the drape at rest. At ks = 500, the cloth hugs the sphere with narrow folds that fall nearly straight down. At the default 5000, the folds are rounder and flare slightly. At 50000, the cloth extends farther to the sides and forms wider folds with raised edges.

Part 4: Handling Self-Collisions

We rebuild a spatial hash after each Verlet update. The cell widths are three times the cloth width and height divided by their respective point counts, with the larger of those two values used along z. We divide the three coordinates by these cell dimensions, round down with floor(), and combine the resulting integer coordinates as bx × 1132 + by × 113 + bz.

For each free mass we inspect the points in its own bucket. A neighbor at a positive distance less than twice the cloth thickness contributes an outward correction equal to the missing separation, directed along the line between the two points. We average the corrections and divide by the number of substeps per frame before applying the result. This spreads the response across substeps in dense folds. Contacts across cell boundaries can be missed because the search only checks the current bucket.

In selfCollision.json, the sheet starts vertical and the bottom edge hits the plane first. The lower layers bunch up while the upper sheet keeps falling. The later capture shows the sheet spread over the plane with a ridge where the remaining layers overlap.

Density and stiffness comparisons

We ran selfCollision.json with the same initial perturbation, camera, and 0.2% damping in all four cases. Each image shows frame 60 (0.67 seconds of simulated time at 90 frames per second and 30 substeps per frame). The lower-density and higher-stiffness runs have broad, parallel folds that reach about 0.27 scene units above the plane. In the higher-density and lower-stiffness runs, the visible folds are narrower and more irregular, and reach only about 0.15 scene units. The two similar pairs share the same ratio of ks to density, which sets the spring acceleration for a given deformation.

Plane penetration also contributes to the low profile. About 60% of the point masses are already below the plane at frame 60 in the higher-density and lower-stiffness runs. By frame 120, both sheets have passed completely through it. The plane handler requires a strict sign change, so a mass that starts exactly on the plane can slip below it and continue downward on the same side.

Part 5: Shaders

A shader program links GPU stages. The vertex shader transforms the mesh vertices and writes their clip-space positions to gl_Position. It also passes world-space positions, normals, texture coordinates, and tangents for the shaders that use them. The rasterizer interpolates those attributes across each triangle. The fragment shader uses the interpolated values and shared uniforms such as the light, camera, or texture to compute a color for each fragment.

Diffuse and Blinn–Phong shading

The diffuse shader uses Lambertian shading. With a unit normal n and a unit direction l to the light, the diffuse strength is proportional to max(0, n · l). Its intensity falls off as the inverse square of the distance to the point light. Blinn–Phong adds a constant ambient term to keep unlit regions visible and a specular term for the highlight. The specular term uses the normalized halfway vector h between the light and view directions. It peaks when n aligns with h, and the exponent of 100 makes that highlight narrow.

In Phong.frag, the ambient, diffuse, and specular coefficients are 0.1, 1.0, and 0.5, respectively. The GUI color tints the ambient and diffuse terms. The specular term keeps the light's color. For each isolated render, the fragment output contains only the corresponding term.

Texture mapping

The cloth mesh assigns normalized UVs across its initial grid. As the vertices move, the UVs stay attached to the mesh. Texture.frag uses each fragment's interpolated UV to sample u_texture_1 and outputs the sampled RGB with full opacity. The floral image in textures/texture_1.png follows the folds as the cloth wraps the sphere.

Bump and displacement mapping

Both shaders use the same custom height map of staggered raised dots through the second texture slot. The bump fragment shader reads height from the red channel and samples a texel ahead in u and v to estimate its local slopes. After applying the height and normal scales, it builds the local normal (−dU, −dV, 1) and transforms it with the tangent, bitangent, and normal basis. Blinn–Phong then uses that perturbed normal to shade apparent relief on the original geometry.

The displacement vertex shader additionally moves each vertex along its original normal by the sampled height times u_height_scaling. The fragment shader still perturbs the normal for lighting. Displacement alters the profile as well as the lighting, so it depends on whether the mesh has enough vertices to sample each raised dot.

With -o 16 -a 16, the bump shader still shows individual dots because it samples per fragment. The sphere outline shows the coarse mesh. Coarse displacement produces an angular, uneven contour because few vertices land on each dot. With -o 128 -a 128, the bump sphere outline becomes rounder and its pattern stays similar. The finer displacement mesh traces the raised dots more closely, with smaller scallops visible around the silhouette.

Environment-mapped reflections

Mirror.frag uses the direction from the surface to the camera as the outgoing view vector. It reflects the negative of that vector around the surface normal and uses the result to sample the environment cubemap. The cloth's changing normals bend the reflected lines around the folds, while the sphere wraps the same environment smoothly over its curved surface.

Extra credit — Iridescent silk shader

The custom shader in Custom.vert and Custom.frag is designed to give the cloth a silk-like sheen with colors that shift as the camera moves. The fragment shader flips the geometric normal toward the viewer on back-facing cloth, then applies a scaled bump perturbation. Three phase-shifted cosine curves generate a thin-film color from the view angle, local height, and elapsed time. A tiled grayscale sample from the color texture adds a faint pattern to the base color.

The custom shader uses wrapped diffuse lighting, tints the specular highlight, and adds a soft sheen and a cubemap reflection that grow stronger at grazing angles. Exponential tone mapping compresses the brightest parts. The two renders below use the same camera and cloth positions from sphere.json after four simulated seconds. The Blinn–Phong baseline shows gray folds with narrow white highlights. With Iridescent Silk, the crown and fold edges pick up rainbow bands, and the broader panels show a faint textile pattern.
